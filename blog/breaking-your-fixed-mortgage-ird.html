Breaking Your Fixed Mortgage:
3 Months' Interest vs. the IRD

Nov 18, 2026  ·  6 min read  ·  By Leo Falkovsky

Nobody signs a five-year fixed mortgage planning to break it. Then life happens. You sell to move closer to family, you separate, you want to pull equity out for a renovation or an investment, or rates drop and refinancing looks tempting. That's when most homeowners find out what their penalty clause actually says.

With a fixed-rate mortgage, the penalty is usually the greater of two numbers: three months' interest, or something called the interest rate differential (IRD). Which one applies, and how your lender calculates it, can be the difference between a few thousand dollars and a five-figure bill.

Three Months' Interest: The Simple One

This one is easy to estimate. Take your balance, multiply by your rate, and divide by four.

Illustration: a $400,000 balance at 4.79% works out to about $4,790 in three months' interest. (Illustrative numbers only.)

Variable-rate mortgages are usually limited to three months' interest, which is one reason I like them for clients who think they may move or restructure within the term.

The IRD: Where the Big Penalties Come From

The IRD is meant to cover the interest your lender loses when you leave early and they have to re-lend your money at today's lower rate. In its simplest form, it's the difference between your rate and the lender's current rate for the time left on your term, applied to your balance for the remaining years.

Illustration: same $400,000 at 4.79%, with 2.5 years left. If the comparison rate is 3.89%, the gap is 0.90%. That's roughly 0.90% × $400,000 × 2.5 years = about $9,000, almost double the three-month figure.

And that's the gentle version. Many lenders, especially the big banks, use their posted rates in the calculation and subtract the discount you received when you signed. That can make the gap much wider than the simple example above, and on a large balance the penalty can climb well past what most people expect.

Why Two "Identical" Mortgages Can Have Very Different Penalties

Two lenders can offer the same rate and the same term, yet charge wildly different penalties for the same situation. The difference is buried in the fine print:

  • Which comparison rate they use: posted rate minus your discount, or the rate they're actually offering today.
  • How they treat the remaining term: whether they round to the nearest term they offer.
  • Whether prepayments count: some lenders let you apply your annual prepayment privilege first, which shrinks the balance the penalty is calculated on.

This is why I always look at the penalty clause before I look at the rate. A rate that's a few basis points lower is not a bargain if the exit door is expensive.

Ways to Shrink or Avoid the Penalty

  1. Port the mortgage. If you're selling and buying, many mortgages let you take your rate and balance to the new home, often with no penalty.
  2. Blend and extend. If you need more money, some lenders will blend your current rate with today's rate on the new amount instead of charging a penalty.
  3. Use your prepayment privilege first. Paying down the maximum allowed before breaking can reduce the balance the penalty applies to.
  4. Time it. As you get closer to maturity, the IRD usually shrinks. Sometimes waiting a few months saves real money.
  5. Ask for the calculation in writing. Get the penalty quote and the method used before you make any decision.

How I Help Clients in Barrie and Simcoe County

When a client calls me about breaking their mortgage, whether it's a move from Barrie to Innisfil or a refinance to consolidate debt, I start by pulling the actual penalty from the lender. Then we compare the options side by side: break and refinance, port, blend, or wait. Sometimes breaking is clearly worth it. Often there's a cheaper path nobody mentioned.
